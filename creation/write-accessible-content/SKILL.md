# Write accessible content

Make information perceivable and navigable by a broad range of readers, including people using assistive technology. Treat accessibility as part of content design, while distinguishing editorial checks from checks that require a rendered page or assistive technology.

## Write and structure for access

- Use descriptive headings in a meaningful hierarchy. Write link text that makes sense in context and when encountered on its own; avoid relying on “click here”.
- Prefer direct, specific language and explain necessary technical terms. Avoid stereotypes, demeaning euphemisms and unexamined assumptions about disability, age, gender or identity. Follow the person's or community's stated language preference when known.
- Give images alternative text that conveys the image's purpose in its surrounding context. Do not repeat nearby text or list decorative details. For complex diagrams, provide an equivalent explanation in the document.
- Do not convey distinctions through color, position, shape or visual styling alone. Add text, labels, patterns or symbols that communicate the same information.
- Keep text and meaningful visual elements sufficiently distinguishable from their background. Do not claim a contrast ratio without measuring the rendered colors and applicable standard.
- Consider how a reader navigates and consumes the content, including heading structure, descriptive links, and equivalent text for visual information.

## Verify within the available evidence

Review the content and structure directly. If rendering, color values, responsive behavior, keyboard access or screen-reader behavior matters, inspect or test that experience with available tools; otherwise state that those checks remain unverified. Do not infer accessibility from source text alone. Respect the user's requested language and preserve exact technical identifiers.
